The hidden work

What one hour in the room really takes

43hours behind every hour

Design and development time for one hour of instructor-led training

On average, organisations spend about 43 hours designing and developing each hour of classroom training, from analysis and objectives to activities, materials and facilitator notes. Simple content sits near 22 hours; complex, customised programmes reach 82.

Source: Chapman Alliance, “How long does it take to create learning?”, survey of 249 organisations, 2010.

How we design · ADDIE

The backbone of instructional design, run at today’s speed

ADDIE was developed at Florida State University in the 1970s and remains the foundation of most instructional design models used today. We follow its five phases for every programme.

  1. AnalyseWe start from your core problem and what success looks like, then find the causes. Sometimes the answer is not training at all, and we will tell you.
  2. DesignClear objectives for each level of your people, the flow of the day, and how we will measure change.
  3. DevelopScenarios, role plays, activities, participant materials and a detailed facilitator guide.
  4. ImplementA recorded train-the-trainer, then delivery by your named trainer, in person or online.
  5. EvaluateFeedback on the day, then behaviour checks at 30, 60 and 90 days, and a free refresher if scores have not moved.

Is ADDIE out of date? Run strictly in sequence, it can be slow. So we keep its discipline but work the way modern design teams do, in short build-test-improve loops, the idea behind the Successive Approximation Model (SAM). You see a working draft early, and we refine it with you.

How we set goals · Bloom’s taxonomy

From knowing to doing

Bloom’s taxonomy, revised in 2001, describes six levels of learning, each building on the last. Many programmes stop at the first two. Ours are designed to reach the levels where behaviour changes. Here is how one skill, giving feedback, climbs the levels.

What the research says

Why we practise, and why we measure

People learn more by doing than by listening

A review of 225 studies found that students taught by lecture alone were 1.5 times more likely to fail than students in courses with active learning, and active learning raised exam scores by about 6%. The studies were in university science courses, but the principle carries into every training room: people learn by doing.

Source: Freeman and others, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2014.

The measures that matter most are used least

The Kirkpatrick model measures training at four levels: reaction, learning, behaviour and results. 92% of organisations measure reaction, yet only 36% of them rate it as highly valuable. Behaviour and results are rated most valuable, by 75%. So we build behaviour and results checks into every programme, at 30, 60 and 90 days.

Source: ASTD and i4cp, “The Value of Evaluation”, survey of 704 business, HR and learning leaders, 2009.

Learning does not end when the session does

The 70-20-10 guide, from research with successful executives, suggests most development comes from experience and other people, not courses. It is a rule of thumb rather than an exact ratio, but it is why our programmes end with real habits to practise at work, manager involvement and follow-ups.

Source: Lombardo and Eichinger, Center for Creative Leadership, 1996.

Theatre-based training

Leadership, rehearsed

Actors rehearse before opening night. Pilots train in simulators. Yet managers are expected to handle their hardest conversations with no practice at all. Theatre-based training gives them a safe stage to try, fail, rewind and try again.

Safe to get it wrongPeople practise the hard moment, a difficult conversation or tough feedback, where mistakes cost nothing.
Remembered, because it was feltLearning that involves emotion and action stays with people longer than slides they read.
Everyone takes partTeam scenes and scoring keep every person involved, from frontline teams to CXOs.
Ready for Monday morningEach person leaves with one real conversation planned and rehearsed.
Forum theatre

A short scene from your workplace plays out. The audience stops it, steps in and changes what happens.

Role reversal

The manager plays the team member, and feels the conversation from the other side.

Pause and rewind

We freeze a scene, look at what just happened, and replay it a better way.

Rehearsal for reality

Participants bring a real situation they face and rehearse it with coaching.

Team games and scoring

Teams compete across activities and quizzes, so energy and attention stay high all day.

Debrief to habits

Every activity ends by turning what happened into one simple habit to use at work.

01Warm up

Teams formed, names chosen, voices and bodies loosened.

02The scene

A real workplace moment, played out in front of the group.

03Step in

Participants stop the scene and try a different approach.

04Rehearse

05Debrief
